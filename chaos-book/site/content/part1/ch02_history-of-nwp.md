## Overview

Richardson computed a six-hour forecast by hand in 1922 and got a surface-pressure change two orders of magnitude too large. He was not wrong about the method — he was defeated by an initial state that was not in balance, and the field needed three decades to understand why. This chapter traces the line from Bjerknes' statement of forecasting as an initial-value problem, through Richardson's failure, to the 1950 ENIAC barotropic forecasts and the growth of operational skill that followed.

## The planned notebook

A minimal shallow-water column initialised out of balance: spurious gravity waves swamp the meteorological signal exactly as they did for Richardson, and vanish when the same state is initialised in geostrophic balance.
